# Examples and practice scenarios

These fictional examples illustrate decisions. They are not default priorities for a particular profession.

## Office work

Input: A client proposal is due at noon and needs 45 minutes of work. There are 20 routine messages and an optional slide redesign. The user has 90 minutes before meetings.

Useful recommendation: Finish the proposal in the first focus block. Define completion as a reviewed proposal ready for submission, unless sending is also authorized. Batch routine messages afterward and defer the redesign. An email count alone does not determine business importance.

## Operations

Input: An active equipment problem is interrupting production. A weekly report is due tomorrow. The user must remain reachable for incident coordination.

Useful recommendation: Prioritize the authorized incident response step or escalation, preserve coverage, and define a checkpoint appropriate to the user's role. Do not recommend silencing essential alerts. Schedule the report after the incident checkpoint or suggest coverage if its deadline becomes at risk.

## Blocked project

Input: A launch decision has the largest business impact, but requires another team's test results. Those results are expected tomorrow. A training checklist can be completed today.

Useful recommendation: Keep the launch decision high priority but blocked. Clarify which result is needed and propose a follow-up point. If a follow-up is needed now, prepare it within scope; otherwise complete the training checklist. Do not repeatedly inspect unchanged evidence or claim the launch decision is complete.

## Personal project

Input: The user wants to finish a professional certification application, estimates two hours, and has only 20 minutes with low energy today.

Useful recommendation: Choose a small application step, such as collecting the required documents and identifying missing items. Define that step's finish separately from submitting the application. Suggest a later focus block for the remaining work rather than claiming everything will fit today.

## Conflicting commitments

Input: Two required deliverables each need three hours, both are due today, and the user has four hours available. Their manager explicitly named the first deliverable the priority.

Useful recommendation: Preserve that priority, explain the two-hour shortfall, and suggest a specific scope reduction, extension, or reassignment discussion for the other deliverable. Do not invent extra capacity or contact the manager without authorization.

## Delegation and elimination

Input: A required audit response can be prepared by a colleague; a duplicate optional report has no known readers.

Useful recommendation: Propose delegation of the audit response while preserving its importance, deadline, and review responsibility. Propose checking whether the duplicate report can stop. Labels D and E do not authorize reassignment or deletion.
